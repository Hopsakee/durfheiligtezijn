Target: `plan-heiligen-welkomstspel.md` (including the new game format) and `plan-review-comments.json`.
Scope: the rubric and matching flow, and the plan measured against its own goal: *8 thirteen-year-olds leave thinking "dit was leuk, ik hoor erbij, ik kom terug".* Hosting, auth and stack were out of scope.

Method: the RedTeam ParallelAnalysis workflow. ## What survived the attack (strengths, near-unanimous)

- **C8, choose-and-explain, is the strongest part of the plan** (5 of 7). It works even when the matching is mediocre, because the kid is the final judge.
- **The face-saving mechanics hold for the guessers**: group-only points, vote counts without names (4 of 7).
- **The LLM containment is right**: curated set only, facts only from the record, JSON validation, deterministic fallback (2 of 7).

## Findings, ranked

### 1. The game is long and mostly passive — high (4 of 7: youth worker, game designer, psychologist, devil's intern)
Eight identical rounds of 7–10 minutes; in each round seven kids watch one. Realistic count (two discussions, the kid leaving twice, up to 7 questions, two votes) is closer to 9–10 minutes per round, so 75–85 minutes of core game with no change of rhythm. Failure mode: boredom from round 4, and the last kid plays after waiting over an hour.
**Fixes proposed:** rounds of ~5 minutes (fewer questions, e.g. max 3–4); a short active break after round 4; give spectators a stake (e.g. a bonus for predicting the group vote).

### 2. The kid being guessed is exposed — high (2 of 7, psychologist strongest)
The scoring protects the guessers, not the kid in the spotlight: sent out twice while peers discuss them, then questioned by seven peers they barely know, in a 5:3 boy-heavy group. A boy with Clara or Thérèse is exposed by the reveal itself.
**Fixes proposed:** anyone may pass on any question; volunteers go first and shy kids in the middle; at "keuze uit drie" the kid may mark a saint as "not for the public round".

### 3. No degraded mode if the quiz or app fails — critical per the devil's intern, high per two others (3 of 7)
The afternoon depends on all 8 kids finishing a live LLM-backed quiz by Thursday, behind a login, and keeping it secret. Two missing kids and the board does not work; the plan has no fallback game.
**Fixes proposed:** a fallback that needs neither app nor LLM (printed board, paper slips or hands up); the quiz must be completable at the start of the afternoon; send reminders.

### 4. The LLM personal explanation is unchecked on a new model — high (1 of 7, but the worst single outcome named)
Prompts were written for Claude; the model is now Gemini. Validation checks structure and saint IDs, not tone or what is quoted. A sad or joking open answer quoted back warmly, and shown in the room, is a one-time public humiliation.
**Fixes proposed:** Jelle reads the 8 final explanations before Saturday; code-level filters (max length, no quoting open answers beyond a few words); test both prompts on Gemini with ~10 adversarial inputs (sad, insulting, empty, injection) before Friday; cap calls per session in code.

### 5. The rubric measures little, and the simulation cannot show it does — high (3 of 7: psychometrician, catechist, devil's intern)
Eight questions over 12 dimensions is under one item per dimension, so the profile is mostly noise from which questions were picked. Social desirability pushes 13-year-olds toward "leader, brave, funny", so the same few saints may win for everyone. Thin Wikipedia articles score as zeros (absence of evidence scored as absence of trait), and cosine on sparse vectors then penalizes those saints, many of them women. Several dimensions overlap (Moed / Opkomen voor eerlijk / Doorzetten). The simulation's fictional profiles are built by the same author as the weights, so it proves spread, not that a real kid recognizes the result.
**Fixes proposed:** forced-choice pairs ("liever X dan Y") instead of single desirable options; store "unknown" as missing, not 0; consider merging overlapping dimensions; test with 3–5 real people (the family rehearsal) rating their top-3 "klopt / klopt niet".

### 6. A temperament rubric reduces holiness to personality — high for faithfulness, low for the goal (1 of 7, catechist)
The 12 dimensions describe what a saint was like, not why the Church holds them up. Risk: "I'm the sporty one, so I'm Frassati". For saints whose death is the point (Kolbe, Edith Stein, Brandsma, Jeanne d'Arc) one sentence flattens them.
**Fixes proposed:** leave the rubric alone; add to every record one thing the saint gave their life to and one struggle, in teen language; reveal framing "jij en Clara delen X; wat Clara bijzonder maakt is Y"; two honest, non-graphic sentences where death is central, read by Jelle or a catechist.

### 7. Feasibility in five days — raised by 5 of 7
Recorded for completeness. The principal decided on 2026-10-06 to build everything; the fallback in finding 3 is what protects the afternoon if the build runs late.

## Steelman

**The position:** a quiz-then-guess game where each kid owns a saint makes a new group feel known.

1. A kid who picks a saint and says why is the author, not the subject, of the match.
2. Group-only scoring and anonymous vote counts remove the main fear of 13-year-olds: public embarrassment.
3. Guess-the-person games are proven icebreakers because being guessed right feels like being seen.
4. A curated saint set with fixed records keeps the LLM from inventing facts in front of kids.
5. The deterministic fallback means an API outage degrades the explanation, never stops the game.
6. Real saints with concrete hooks, like Carlo's websites, make faith tangible instead of abstract.
7. Repeating the quiz in June turns one afternoon into a story the group shares over a year.
8. Even an imperfect match works, because the kid's "klopt, want…" sentence carries the meaning.

## Counter-argument

**The position red-teamed:** the plan as written delivers "dit was leuk, ik hoor erbij, ik kom terug".

1. Belonging is measured by the most exposed kid's round, and the plan protects only the guessers.
2. Eight identical seven-minute rounds leave seven of eight kids watching for over an hour.
3. Party games that bore thirteen-year-olds by round four are remembered as boring, whatever the reveal.
4. Eight questions over twelve dimensions measure question choice more than the kid behind the answers.
5. The simulation checks spread on invented profiles, so passing it says nothing about recognition.
6. A warm LLM quoting a sad open answer back, on an untested model, can humiliate one kid publicly.
7. Every element hangs on all eight kids finishing a live app quiz, with no paper fallback.
8. The mechanic is sound; the risk is the wrapping, and it needs a degraded mode and shorter rounds.

**Assessment:** fundamentally sound in its core mechanic (choose, explain, be guessed as a group), with fixable execution: shorten and vary the rounds, give the spotlight kid exits, human-gate the LLM text, and keep a no-app fallback.

## Principal's response, 2026-10-06

- **Finding 1 rejected.** All seven kids discuss and vote in every round, so they are not spectators; the "seven watch one" framing was wrong. Rounds are ~5 minutes; the plan's timetable now says so.
- **Finding 2 accepted, differently.** The kid stays in the room; seeing them smile is part of the game, not a leak.
- **Finding 3 accepted.** A printed version of the game is prepared.
- **Finding 4 mostly void.** It attacked a leftover line in the plan (a TV variant of the explanation for the old hint format). The explanation is shown only to the kid on their own phone, and Jelle reads all of them before the afternoon. The plan is corrected.
- **Finding 5 accepted.** Either-or questions; the rubric itself is open for revision.
- **Finding 6 accepted, scoped.** Each saint record says why the saint is an example; matching stays on character traits, not on holiness.
